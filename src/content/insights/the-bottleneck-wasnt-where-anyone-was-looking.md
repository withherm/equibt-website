## What we did, and what we found.

A federal government processing centre handling benefit eligibility assessments had a persistent backlog of 11,200 cases. The assessment queue was the most visible pain point: cases sat for an average of 23 days waiting for an assessor. The operations director commissioned an improvement programme targeting assessment throughput.

Over nine months, the team redesigned assessor workflows, introduced batch processing for straightforward cases, and implemented a triage system to prioritise urgent applications. Assessment throughput increased by 22 percent. At the 12-month review, the backlog stood at 10,800 cases. Almost no change.

The improvement team had delivered exactly what was asked. But the backlog was not caused by slow assessment. Analysis traced the rework to intake classification, the third step in a nine-step process, where 41 percent of cases had been misclassified and needed to be rerouted, returned for correction, or reassessed under the wrong criteria. Every misclassified case consumed assessment capacity twice.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Optimising the step where pain is most visible, not the step where the constraint originates.**

Assessment was where the backlog was visible. It was where managers could see cases stacking up, where assessors were clearly overloaded, and where complaints about delays were loudest. It was the natural target. But the reason assessment was overloaded was not that assessors were slow. It was that 41 percent of their workload was rework generated by upstream misclassification. Improving assessor throughput by 22 percent meant each assessor processed more cases per day. They were processing rework faster. They were not reducing the volume of rework entering the queue.

This is a consistent pattern in high-volume processing environments. The most visible bottleneck, the step with the largest queue, the longest wait time, the most complaints, is almost always downstream. The constraint that generates the queue is upstream, often invisible because it does not have a queue of its own. Intake classification had no backlog. Cases flowed through it quickly. The fact that 41 percent of them flowed into the wrong stream was not visible until someone traced the rework back to its origin.
