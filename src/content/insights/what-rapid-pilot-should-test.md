## What we did, and what we found.

A utilities company piloted a new field service scheduling process in one region for 6 weeks. All metrics were positive. Full rollout began across 4 regions. Three months in, performance in the other 3 regions was significantly below pilot results.

Investigation revealed the cause. The pilot region had a system configuration that automated one scheduling step, a configuration that did not exist in the other three regions. The pilot had tested whether the design worked in ideal conditions. It had not tested whether those conditions existed at scale. The answer was no.

There was a second problem. The pilot had run with strong team members and high engagement from management. Staff invested time making the pilot succeed. At scale, the process ran in normal conditions with average effort. The pilot had demonstrated what the design could do with exceptional effort, not what it would do with normal effort.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Designing the pilot to confirm the hypothesis rather than to challenge it.**

A pilot that only asks does it work will confirm whatever conditions it was run in. The design team set up the pilot in conditions favourable to the design. At scale, conditions are different. System variations, capability levels, volume spikes, and exception rates are not the same. When the design hits real conditions it underperforms, and the gap is not visible until full rollout.

The most useful pilots are the ones that find a problem before the rollout does. That requires designing the pilot to specifically stress the areas most likely to fail, not the areas most likely to succeed. A pilot that passes is not necessarily a green light. It is only a green light if it passed on the conditions that actually exist at scale.
