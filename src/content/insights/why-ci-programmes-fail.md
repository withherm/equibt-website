## What we did, and what we found.

A state government agency ran a continuous improvement programme for 18 months with strong visible results. Cycle times dropped 35 percent. Rework fell. Staff morale improved. The programme manager and external support team moved on. Six months later, an internal investigation found something unsettling. Cycle times had drifted back by 60 percent of the gains made. The improvement methods were no longer being used.

The post-mortem was revealing. The team leads had learned to participate in improvement cycles when they were run by the programme manager. They had not learned to diagnose variation in their own process. When the programme closed, there was no one left who could spot when the process was drifting, and no methodology embedded in the team's day-to-day work to correct it.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Measuring CI programme success by metrics at close, not by what the team can do independently 12 months later.**

In most CI programmes, the external support team designs the methodology, runs the cycles, and coaches the team. The team participates. The metrics improve. But the team is learning to follow a framework, not to diagnose and improve their own process. The knowledge is in the hands of the person running the session, not distributed across the people who will maintain it.

This is not a failure of the team. It is a design failure in how the engagement is structured. If the measure of success is the metrics at programme close, the programme looks successful. If the measure is what the team does 12 months after the external support leaves, it is not. The real question is whether the team can diagnose their own drift, identify the root cause, and fix it without external help.
