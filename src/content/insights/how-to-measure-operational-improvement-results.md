## What we did, and what we found.

A government department closed an improvement programme and reported A$1.4 million in annual savings to the efficiency review board. The savings looked strong. Six months later, an independent review was conducted. The baseline had been estimated, not measured. No observation of the baseline process had occurred before work started. The post-improvement measurement used a different methodology: baseline used transaction cost times volume; post-improvement used staff time times hourly rate.

When both measures were rerun using consistent methodology, validated savings were A$380,000, still significant but 27 cents in the dollar of what had been reported. The credibility of the programme was damaged. Two subsequent improvement proposals were rejected. The savings opportunity stayed on the table because the team could not secure approval for another initiative.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Estimating the baseline rather than measuring it before the engagement starts.**

Benefits measurement fails three consistent ways. First, the baseline is estimated rather than measured. Quick and cheap at the start, but an estimate is not evidence. Second, the post-improvement measure uses a different methodology to the baseline, not always deliberately, but because the person designing improvement did not work with whoever measured the baseline. Third, nobody is assigned ongoing responsibility for tracking whether the improvement holds. Tracking stops when the project closes.

All three failures are preventable if measurement is designed at the start, not retrofitted at close. An estimated baseline produces a disputed result at close. A methodology mismatch makes the comparison invalid. Unassigned ownership means the improvement does not survive beyond project close. The only protection is a signed measurement agreement at the start that specifies the baseline figure, the method, and the name of the person responsible for tracking it.
