## What we did, and what we found.

A bank's mortgage discharge team had been improving the same process for three years. Cycle time had moved from 45 days to 31 days across four improvement cycles. The market standard was 5 days. The process was getting better. The bank was still losing competitive ground.

The diagnostic revealed the design constraint. The process required 14 mandatory handoffs across six teams. Each handoff existed not because anyone was slow, but because the design demanded it: property manager to legal reviewer to compliance officer to finance and beyond. No amount of improving individual steps would reach five days because the handoffs themselves were load-bearing. The design was the ceiling, not the execution.

The team took the reengineering path. They collapsed the 14 handoffs into a single process owned by one discharge team with clear escalation protocols instead of sequential approval chains. Cycle time moved from 31 days to 7 days.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Running more improvement cycles on a process whose design is the ceiling.**

Processes accumulate constraints incrementally. A new requirement arrives and an approval stage is added. A compliance obligation changes and a handoff is inserted. A team restructure happens and the process follows the org chart rather than the work. Over years these accumulate into designs where most of the cycle time comes from handoffs between teams, not from the work itself.

Improvement is safer. It works within existing structures and does not require difficult ownership conversations. Reengineering requires different accountability: usually a single person or function responsible for the whole process, not a chain of teams each owning a step. But when the improvement ceiling is still uncompetitive, the safer choice is not actually safe. It is the choice that keeps the organisation behind the market standard for another three years while consuming improvement budget on a fundamentally broken design.
