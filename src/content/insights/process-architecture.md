## What we did, and what we found.

A government regulator had thorough SOPs for investigation processes. Two investigators were observed over four weeks. The first escalated 34 percent of matters. The second escalated 6 percent. Same SOPs, same matter types, same team leader.

The word in the escalation rule was "complex." The SOP outlined the standard workflow and explained the escalation rule. It did not define what "complex" meant. Was it about the subject matter? The number of parties involved? The legal risk? The time commitment? Without a definition, "complex" was in the eye of the investigator. Two capable people read the same rule and made different decisions. The team leader was managing inconsistent outputs and spending significant time on matters that did not need escalation, while occasionally missing ones that did.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Documenting the steps without defining the words that determine how people actually decide.**

SOPs describe the happy path. They tell you what to do when things are normal. They do not tell you what to do when they are not. A customer arrives with a special request: process as standard or escalate? The SOP does not say because the SOP assumes standard requests. A transaction fails validation: hold pending review or return it? The SOP does not say because the SOP assumes validation passes.

Without guidance on these decisions, people improvise. Different staff handle the same situation differently. Customers experience different service. Auditors find different procedures. Compliance gaps emerge because nobody knows what the procedure actually is.

Process architecture captures these decisions. It makes explicit what the SOP left implicit. When you have it, a new person can read the architecture and know what to do without asking a manager. Inconsistency becomes visible because it is a deviation from defined rules. Compliance becomes auditable because the rules are documented.
