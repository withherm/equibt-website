## What we did, and what we found.

An energy retailer with 1.2 million residential accounts launched two major programmes within eight weeks of each other. The first was an operational reengineering of billing and collections, targeting a 40 percent reduction in manual touchpoints. The second was an enterprise AI programme to deploy predictive models across customer service, network planning, and billing. Both were approved by the executive committee. Both had dedicated programme directors and separate steering committees.

By September, the reengineering programme was three months behind. The AI programme had completed only one of four planned pilots. A review found 14 subject matter experts were committed to both programmes simultaneously. Workshop attendance was averaging 55 percent because the same people were double booked. The operations team had stopped attending the reengineering sessions to prioritise the AI work, which had more executive visibility.

There was a deeper sequencing problem. The AI team had built a collections propensity model calibrated to the existing process. Three months later, the reengineering team redesigned collections entirely. The model needed to be retrained from scratch. Six months of AI work produced nothing usable.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Treating transformation programmes as independent when they draw from the same finite change capacity.**

Change capacity in any organisation is finite. It is not just budget and headcount. It is the availability of the 30 to 50 people who understand how the operation actually works, who can validate whether a proposed design will function, and whose participation is required for anything to move from concept to implementation. When two programmes compete for that pool without coordination, neither gets enough.

The sequencing issue matters beyond resource competition. Reengineering defines the target operating model. AI enables specific capabilities within that model. AI built on top of a process about to be reengineered automates the wrong state. The right sequence is: reengineer the process to its target state, then automate within that target state.
