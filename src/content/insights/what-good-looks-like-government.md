## What we did, and what we found.

A federal department ran a grants assessment improvement programme. Cycle time fell from 47 days to 25 days. Rework reduced from 34 percent to 8 percent. Capacity was freed up. Twelve months later, the next efficiency review asked the same questions: what is the cycle time now, what is the rework rate? The team could not answer clearly. The gains were real but undocumented as a standard. Without a defined benchmark, there was no way to know if the operation was drifting.

Four conditions determine whether government improvement holds: a sponsor with authority to remove blockers at speed; named ownership with the process owner in the room from the start; measurable objectives with concrete targets rather than intentions; and a written standard at close.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Closing a programme without documenting the improved state as an operational standard.**

In government, improvement without a defined standard has no anchor. The next leadership team inherits the process but not the knowledge of what it should produce. The next efficiency review cannot assess whether performance is holding or drifting. Drift is invisible until it is significant.

Defining good, with specific metrics, acceptable ranges, and review triggers, converts a programme outcome into an operational standard. The programme proved it is possible to run this process at 25 days with 8 percent rework. The standard says that is now how this process runs. The two things are different, and most government improvement programmes produce the first without writing the second.
