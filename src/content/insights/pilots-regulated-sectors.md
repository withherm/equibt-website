## What we did, and what we found.

A health insurer implemented a new complaints handling process and required a pilot in one division before full rollout: 8 weeks, real volume, full compliance monitoring. In week five, a pattern emerged. A specific complaint type arriving via the broker channel was being categorised incorrectly under the new process. This created a Private Health Insurance Ombudsman reporting obligation the team was not aware of.

Fixed in the pilot. If the process had gone live across all divisions simultaneously, the compliance exposure would have been across the full customer book.

The compliance team had been included in pilot design, not just in sign-off. That meant they had already flagged the broker channel as a specific area of risk and monitoring was in place from week one. The pattern was visible in week five because someone was specifically watching for it.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Treating the pilot as an operational validation only, with compliance as a sign-off step.**

In regulated industries, operational failure is not the only downside of a failed process change. A bank changes a loan approval process and consistency drops: exposed on audit. A healthcare provider changes a medication process and errors increase: liability and licensing risk. A government agency changes a permit process and misses statutory timelines: breach of legislation. In each case, discovery at full scale is catastrophic.

The pilot is the testing ground under conditions that simulate real regulatory scrutiny. It is not just operational validation; it is compliance validation under controlled conditions. When compliance is involved only at sign-off, the pilot measures whether the design works faster. When compliance is involved at design, the pilot measures whether the design maintains compliance across real exceptions. Those are different questions, and only the second one is the right question in a regulated environment.
