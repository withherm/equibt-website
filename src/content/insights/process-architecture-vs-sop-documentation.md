## What we did, and what we found.

A water utility had 400 SOPs. Three years of documentation work. Every standard process described step by step, clear and accurate. Then a team leader with nine years in the role went on extended leave. Within three weeks, the operation degraded. Water usage queries took longer to resolve. Billing disputes were not escalated correctly. Some customers were not receiving refunds.

The team was following the SOPs. The SOPs were not enough. Investigation revealed why. The SOPs described the standard path perfectly. But they did not capture what happens when a customer has both a billing dispute and a disconnection order simultaneously, or who had authority to approve an exception payment, or what constituted a matter requiring escalation. Those decisions had lived in one person's head. The team had a rulebook. They did not have a decision framework.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Documenting steps without documenting the decisions that govern non-standard conditions.**

SOPs are useful for standard conditions. When everything is normal, SOPs work. But operations spend significant time in non-standard conditions. Customers with unusual requests. Processes that interact in unanticipated ways. Data that is missing or incorrect. When the non-standard arrives, the team needs to know how to decide, not just which steps to follow.

Process architecture makes decision rules explicit. It names decision points, specifies the criteria that drive each decision, names who is accountable, and specifies what to do when criteria are ambiguous. The institutional knowledge that sustains an operation is not the day-to-day process. It is the judgment that comes from experience: knowing when to escalate, what to prioritise, what flexibility exists within the rules. Process architecture captures that judgment and makes it explicit.
