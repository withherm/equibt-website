## What we did, and what we found.

An energy retailer had designed a new retention model through six workshops. The model was sound. Economics worked. Leadership approved rollout across three call centres and 400 agents. We recommended six weeks in one centre first. Leadership was sceptical. The pilot was approved anyway.

Week three revealed the gaps. The model worked and churn reduction matched projections. But it failed for customers who had lodged formal complaints. These customers needed the complaint addressed first, not a retention offer. The case affected 8% of interactions and the design workshop had not anticipated it. A second gap emerged: agents beyond five years tenure were drifting from the framework, reverting to old approaches. This was not a training failure. It was a supervision gap. Experienced agents needed active coaching, not classroom training.

Both gaps were fixed by week four. The complaint pathway was redesigned. The team leader role was restructured to include weekly coaching for experienced agents. By week six, the model was solid. Full rollout across three centres ran clean.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Running a pilot that only confirms your hypothesis is not evidence. It is rehearsal.**

Design workshops work in ideal conditions. People are fresh. Thinking is collaborative. Scenarios are resolved logically. The model is sound on paper. But real operations have volume, variation, and pressure. People are interrupted. Exceptions arrive that nobody anticipated. The model that worked in theory meets the person who does not fit the model.

A pilot is not a rehearsal of a finished design. It is the final stage of design. The edge cases that emerge are never the ones the design team predicted. Define what the pilot needs to prove before it starts, and the go or no-go decision becomes objective.
