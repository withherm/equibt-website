## What we did, and what we found.

A utilities company improved their complaint resolution process over 9 months. Cycle time down from 52 days to 31. Backlog reduced by 58 percent. At an 18-month review, cycle time was back to 44 days.

The post-mortem identified three failure points. The programme manager who owned the measurement left at month 10 and nobody picked it up. During implementation, cycle time was tracked daily and everyone could see progress. Three months after close, that measurement had dropped to monthly. The monthly number masked variation. The team had no feedback loop and no way to know the process was drifting until it was already 13 days off target.

The team had been participating in improvement cycles but not leading them. When external support ended, the problem-solving responsibility reverted to whoever held it before, on top of their existing workload. The cycles stopped. The improved process had never been documented as the new standard, so variation crept back in without anyone noticing it was a deviation.

## The pattern to avoid.

**Designing the improvement engagement to deliver results without designing it for sustainability.**

Each failure point is preventable, but each is a design decision that must be made at programme start. Measurement ownership tied to the programme disappears when the programme does. Capability tied to the consultant leaves with the consultant. Without documentation of the improved state, there is nothing to compare against when drift begins.

Most improvement programmes deliver results. Most of those results are gone within twelve months. The reason is almost always the same: the programme was designed to deliver improvement, not to leave the team able to sustain it. Improvement is an outcome. Sustainability is the design decision that was made, or not made, before the work started.
